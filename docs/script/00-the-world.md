# The world

> The reference, not the script. What is true of the kingdom before act 1
> begins, written as fact rather than as scene, so the acts can be checked
> against it. The player learns all of this the slow way, in the story.
>
> Everything here is established by acts 1 to 3. Act 4 (The Gates) is still an
> open outline, and what it owes is listed under [Open](#open) at the end.
>
> First-person narration throughout the script.

## The kingdom

A castle, and by the look of it the whole of a kingdom: courtyards, walls,
rooftops, a clock tower across the courtyard, and one great gate, shut. There
is a palace with corridors far too long to belong to a house, portraits, and a
bedroom with a bed that has not been slept in for a very long time.

It is abandoned, and abandoned long enough for the grass to take the courtyard — how long exactly is not established, and nobody in it knows. Dust on everything, mildew
in the tapestry, webs thick enough to bind a man's arm to a handrail, grass
knee-high in the courtyard with the stones showing only where the path was most
trodden, a well with its rope rotted through, a wheelbarrow rusted the color of
dirt, ivy working into the cracks of the tower. The clock on the tower's face is
stopped and the paint of its numbers is flaking.

Nothing moves and nobody is awake, and the quiet includes the things that should
never be quiet: in a bell tower, silence is wrong.

## You

You hold the office — King or Queen, the player's choice — and it is your
kingdom, the whole of it. You wake on the bedroom floor, face down, far too
late, in the room where you were supposed to wake. You carry the chronicle from
the moment you find it.

The others know you on sight and address you by the office. Nobody has to be
told who you are.

## The offices

Each office has one person and one object, and the object is the office.

| Office             | Runs         | Object                | What the object holds                       |
| ------------------ | ------------ | --------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| yours              | the kingdom  | **the chronicle**     | everything that happens, recorded by itself |
| **Steward**        | the errands  | **the day's roll**    | what to do, with no hour set                |
| **Herald of Time** | the hours    | **the book of hours** | what has an hour: start, duration, end      |
| **Gatekeeper**     | what arrives | parcels and notes     | what comes from outside and waits           |

The Steward keeps the palace and attends you there. The Herald of Time keeps the
clock tower, your hours and your appointments — whom you were to see and when.
The Gatekeeper keeps the gate and sees everything that comes in and everything
that goes out; if anything passed these walls, it passed through him.

Each of them, at the end of his own act, sends you to the next: the Steward to
the Herald, the Herald to the Gatekeeper.

## The objects and their laws

The objects are ordinary to everyone who works here. They are not magic, they
are how the kingdom is run, and they have been here longer than the people
staffing it — the chronicle predates the Steward.

**The chronicle.** It records everything that happens in the kingdom: what you
do, what changes, what is kept, what is lost. It writes itself, letter by
letter, with no quill and no hand, and it does not stop for being watched. It
asks nothing, begs no leave, and passes no judgment. It records your corrections
too, because the truth of a day includes your having changed your mind partway
through it. It does not fail. It has never failed once.

**The day's roll.** What is to be done, with no hour attached. It is a sheet
clamped to a wooden clipboard, and the Steward carries it to you.

**The book of hours.** The when: what starts, when it starts, how long it lasts,
when it ends. The future is set and unset at will. The past can only be
**corrected** — the book does not always follow closely, and when it notes that
you began at nine and it was in truth ten to nine, you say so and the Herald
puts it right. The chronicle then records that you corrected it.

**The bell.** It rings because it is written in the book of hours. Nobody rings
it. The Herald never did.

**Consent.** The roll and the book of hours **propose**. A line appears by
itself, pale, and sits there until you take it; only then is it yours. Nothing
enters your roll without your consent. The kingdom proposes, and you are the one
who accepts.

## The stopping

Everyone in the kingdom is stopped mid-gesture, exactly where their work had
them: the Steward in the corridor holding out a tray to a person who is not
there, the Herald on a stair with one foot in the air and a hand raised to say
something, the Gatekeeper crouched with his hand on a crate he never lifted. Eyes
open, fixed on nothing. They do not hear, and a hand waved in front of their eyes
does nothing.

The bell is stopped too, tipped mid-swing, with no rope taut and nothing holding
it — stopped where nothing stops. Whatever happened caught the kingdom's things
as well as its people.

## The waking

You wake them, and the way you wake them is to take the object of their office
out of their hand.

They start mid-sentence, like men who never stopped, and they resume the gesture
they were making: the Steward announces the roll, the Herald bows. Waking the
Herald also releases the bell, which rings on a sound that fills the tower and
brings the dust down in a curtain. What was stopped resumes, not only who.

## What the waking keeps and loses

The same gap in everyone, you included. It sits in the near past, not in the
self.

- **Kept.** Who they are. Their office and how it is done. How the kingdom works,
  the chronicle and the book of hours included, which is why none of it strikes
  them as remarkable. And you — name and face, on sight.
- **Lost.** How they got here. What they were doing. How long it has been.
  Anything that happened in this place, up to the moment of waking.
- They do not learn they lost anything until they look around. The Steward is
  courteous and ordinary until he sees the dust and the web on his own sleeve;
  the Herald bows before he understands where he is standing. Then the same
  answer from each: he does not know what happened, and he does not know how much
  time has passed.
- Each reaches for the part of the world he still trusts — the Steward for the
  roll, "it is where we always begin" — and that is what breaks him, because the
  objects are empty.

## The books are empty

This is the wound, and everyone who sees it is frightened by it:

- **The chronicle** is blank backward, page after page, handfuls at a time. The
  book that has never failed once has nothing in it. It begins recording again
  the moment you wake, starting with your waking.
- **The book of hours** is blank backward and forward, and your calendar was
  full — too full, in the Herald's opinion.
- **The day's roll** is blank when the Steward hands it to you, and his answer to
  that is that it cannot be: your day was always full.

The one exception is today's page in the book of hours, marked with a ribbon.
It carries four lines, written beforehand in the infinitive the way you note what
is still to be done, and struck through afterward one by one as you did them:

> ~~Wake up~~
> ~~Find the chronicle~~
> ~~Say my name~~
> ~~Speak with the Steward~~

Today was written in advance. Nobody in the kingdom claims to have written it,
and the Herald's answer is the same bored one he gives about everything else: the
book writes, that is what it is for.

## How the player is named

- The chronicle asks in the bedroom, as a recap and not as a question:
  `to recap, my name is`. You already know the answer — the book is reading it
  back to you. The player writes the name there.
- The title comes with the Steward's first greeting in act 2. He says it the way
  he has said it every morning of his life; the player picks whether it is King
  or Queen.
- The title settles the form of address for the whole script. It is written
  **my lord** throughout, and reads _my lady_ for a Queen.

## Open

Not established yet. Act 4 is where most of it is owed.

- What stopped the kingdom, and how long ago. Nobody who has woken so far knows
  either.
- Whether anyone is awake anywhere else, and what lies past the gate at all.
- Who or what wrote today's page in the book of hours before you woke, and why
  the books are empty when the chronicle cannot fail.
- The last note left before the disappearance, never processed, carrying a
  **warning** rather than a task — something like "i think i am losing control".
  The Gatekeeper had no shelf for that, and it stayed.
- The Gate as the place where everything from outside stops: a note is what you
  write to yourself, a parcel is what arrives from outside, and both wait there.
