# The world

> The reference, not the script. What is true of the kingdom, written as fact
> rather than as scene, so the acts can be checked against it.
>
> **It spoils the game.** The player is meant to work most of this out slowly,
> and the last section — [What the kingdom is](#what-the-kingdom-is) — is the
> answer to the whole thing. Sections marked **(player-facing)** are what the
> game ever says out loud.
>
> Acts 1 to 3 are written and this file follows them. Act 4 (The Gates) is an
> open outline, and what is not settled is listed under [Open](#open) at the
> end.
>
> The script is first-person; this file says "the player" and means the person
> holding the controls and the character they wake up as, which by the end of
> the game is the same admission the game makes.

## The kingdom (player-facing)

A castle, and by the look of it the whole of a kingdom: courtyards, walls,
rooftops, a clock tower across the courtyard, and one great gate, shut. There is
a palace with corridors far too long to belong to a house, portraits, and a
bedroom with a bed that has not been slept in for a very long time.

It is abandoned, and abandoned long enough for the grass to take the courtyard —
how long exactly is not established, and nobody in it knows. Dust on everything,
mildew in the tapestry, webs thick enough to bind a man's arm to a handrail,
grass knee-high with the stones showing only where the path was most trodden, a
well with its rope rotted through, a wheelbarrow rusted the color of dirt, ivy
working into the cracks of the tower. The clock on the tower's face is stopped
and the paint of its numbers is flaking.

Nothing moves and nobody is awake, and the quiet includes the things that should
never be quiet: the bell hangs stopped in the middle of a toll, tipped past the
top of its swing with nothing holding it, and in a bell tower, silence is wrong.

## The player (player-facing)

The player holds the office — King or Queen, their choice — and the kingdom is
theirs, the whole of it. They wake on the bedroom floor, face down, far too
late, and they recognize nothing: not the room, not the castle, not the people
in it.

That is the honest state of a first playthrough and the game does not pretend
otherwise. Everything the player brought from their own life is intact; what
they do not have is this place, because they have never been here. Waking into
it is closer to a dream than to amnesia — they are handed a kingdom they have
never seen and have to learn what it is for.

A second playthrough is a different case: a player who has done this before can
skip the onboarding, and that is tracked separately.

## The offices (player-facing)

Three officers, one building each, and the player over the whole kingdom.
Nothing in the castle belongs to the officers — every object here is the
player's. What each officer does is **introduce** the object they work with, the
first time it matters, and then run the part of the kingdom that keeps it. The
chronicle is the exception: it introduces itself, in the bedroom, before anyone
else says a word.

| Office             | Building        | Introduces            | What it runs                            |
| ------------------ | --------------- | --------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| the player         | the kingdom     | —                     | all of it                               |
| **Steward**        | the palace      | **the day's roll**    | the errands: what to do, with no hour   |
| **Herald of Time** | the clock tower | **the book of hours** | the hours: what starts, lasts, and ends |
| **Gatekeeper**     | the gate        | notes and parcels     | what arrives from outside and waits     |

The Steward attends the player in the palace. The Herald of Time keeps the clock
tower, the hours and the appointments — whom the player was to see and when. The
Gatekeeper keeps the gate and sees everything that comes in and everything that
goes out; if anything passed these walls, it passed through him.

Each of them, at the end of his own act, sends the player to the next: the
Steward to the Herald, the Herald to the Gatekeeper.

## The objects and their laws (player-facing)

Nobody in the kingdom finds the objects remarkable, and nobody can explain them
either. Asked how the chronicle writes itself, the Steward answers the way
anyone answers about the tools of their own century: it is a book, it works, it
has always worked. What looks like magic to the player is this world's
technology, or the nature of the world itself, and the people living in it have
no more account of it than that.

**The chronicle.** It records everything that happens in the kingdom: what is
done, what changes, what is kept, what is lost. It writes in the player's own
voice, in the first person and in lower case, and what it writes is not only
acts — in the bedroom it puts down the thought before the thought is finished.
It writes itself, letter by letter, with no quill and no hand, and it does not
stop for being watched. It asks nothing, begs no leave, and passes no judgment.
It records corrections too, because the truth of a day includes having changed
your mind partway through it.

It is the activity log, and it is meant to be used as one: the player can read
back what was done, when, and by whom, and from there review a particular change
and undo it.

**The day's roll.** What is to be done, with no hour attached — the day's tasks
and quests as they are handed to the player. It is a sheet clamped to a wooden
clipboard, and the Steward carries it in.

**The book of hours.** The when: what starts, when it starts, how long it lasts,
when it ends. It is the calendar, and it holds three kinds of thing — events,
which can come from the player's real calendars (Google Calendar first);
routines, which have a start, an end, and a checklist inside them; and blocked
time for work, for personal things, for whatever the day has to make room for.
Planning the day is moving what the roll proposes into the hours the book keeps.

The future is set and unset at will. The past can only be **corrected** — the
book does not always follow closely, and when it notes a start at nine that was
in truth ten to nine, the player says so and the Herald puts it right. The
chronicle then records that it was corrected.

**The bell.** It rings because it is written in the book of hours. Nobody rings
it, and the Herald never did: it rings when something scheduled starts and again
when it ends. That is the timer — a pomodoro wired into the calendar rather than
standing beside it. Anything with an hour can also be started now instead of
later: a task or quest on the roll moves into the present, the bell rings, and
the count begins; an event or a routine still ahead can be pulled to now the
same way.

**Consent.** The roll **proposes**. A line appears by itself, pale, and sits
there until it is taken; only then is it the player's. Nothing enters the roll
without consent — the kingdom proposes and the player accepts. Proposals come
from what happens in the kingdom, from notes that arrive carrying work, and from
what the officers are asked to do: they are agents, and a request made to one
comes back as a line waiting to be accepted.

## The stopping (player-facing)

What is stopped is not a person but a building, and everything inside it.

In the palace the air itself has quit: no breeze, no sound, neither warm nor
cold, dust hanging in the light exactly where it hangs. From the balcony the
world outside is plainly still running — clouds crossing, leaves turning over in
the road beyond the gate — and none of it reaches in. The player is not told
this and is not meant to notice it on a first pass; it is there to be noticed
later, or on a second playthrough.

The people in a stopped building are stopped where their work had them, eyes
open and fixed on nothing, deaf to a shout and to a hand waved in front of them:
the Steward in the corridor holding a tray out to a person who is not there, the
Herald on the stair coming down with the book under his arm and his free hand
reaching for a rail that is not on that side. The bell is stopped in its toll
with no rope taut and nothing holding it. Whatever happened caught the kingdom's
things as squarely as its people.

## The waking (player-facing)

The player wakes a building by taking up the object its officer was carrying —
the clipboard off the Steward's tray, the book of hours out from under the
Herald's arm. The object is the player's to take; that is why it works.

What resumes is the whole building, not only the man. He starts mid-sentence,
like someone who never stopped, and finishes the gesture he was making: the
Steward announces the roll, the Herald bows. The air comes back with him, and
whatever else was held: waking the Herald releases the bell, which finishes its
toll on a sound that fills the tower and brings the dust down in a curtain.

## What the waking keeps and loses (player-facing)

The same gap in everyone. It sits in the near past, not in the self.

- **Kept.** Who they are. Their office and what they are responsible for — the
  Steward knows he runs the errands, the Herald knows he keeps the hours. How
  the kingdom works, the objects included, which is why none of it strikes them
  as remarkable. And the player — name and face, on sight. The Steward says the
  name at once, without hunting for it.
- **Lost.** How they got here. What was actually on the table: which errands
  were pending, which appointments were coming, what was in hand the moment
  everything stopped. How long it has been. Anything that happened in this
  place, up to the moment of waking.
- **Slow, but not lost: the player's own name.** Reaching for it in the bedroom,
  it is not on top, where it should be. It comes back inside the first scene,
  whole, the way a word you have said all your life comes back. Nobody else has
  this trouble, and nobody else's name is ever in question.
- Nobody learns they lost anything until they look around. The Steward is
  courteous and ordinary until he sees the dust and the web on his own sleeve;
  the Herald bows before he understands where he is standing. Then the same
  answer from each: he does not know what happened, and he does not know how
  much time has passed.
- Each reaches for the part of the world he still trusts — the Steward for the
  roll, "it is where we always begin" — and that is what breaks him, because the
  objects are empty.

## The books are empty (player-facing)

This is the wound, and everyone who sees it is frightened by it:

- **The chronicle** is blank backward, page after page, handfuls at a time. The
  book that has never failed once has nothing in it. It begins recording again
  the moment the player wakes, starting with the waking.
- **The book of hours** is blank backward and forward, and the calendar was full
  — too full, in the Herald's opinion.
- **The day's roll** is blank when the Steward hands it over, and his answer to
  that is that it cannot be: the day was always full.

The one exception is today's page in the book of hours, marked with a ribbon. It
carries four lines, written beforehand in the infinitive the way you note what is
still to be done, and struck through afterward one by one as they were done:

> ~~Wake up~~
> ~~Find the chronicle~~
> ~~Say my name~~
> ~~Speak with the Steward~~

That page is unsettled — see [Open](#open). Written in advance, it is the one
thing in the castle that knew the player was coming.

## How the player is named (player-facing)

- **The name, in the bedroom.** The player's own name is the one thing slow to
  come back, and it comes back within the scene. The chronicle writes the
  hunting for it in their own voice — `wait`, `who am i`, `i am not
remembering. it is here. think` — the player writes the name, and it lands as
  a thing recovered rather than invented: `yes. that is my name`.
- **The title, with the Steward.** He knows the player and says the name without
  hunting, then runs straight on into the office in the same sentence; the
  player picks whether the word is King or Queen.
- The title settles the form of address for the whole script. It is written
  **my lord** throughout, and reads _my lady_ for a Queen.

## What the kingdom is

**Spoilers. This is the answer, and the player is not told any of it until the
end.**

The kingdom is not a place. It is the player's own mind, laid out as somewhere
they can walk: a second brain with a drawbridge. Each officer is a part of it,
the part that keeps one area of a life running — the Steward what has to be
done, the Herald where the hours go, the Gatekeeper what comes in from outside —
and the objects are those areas made into things you can pick up. That is why
nothing in the castle belongs to the staff, why the chronicle writes in the
player's voice, and why an abandoned kingdom is an abandoned life rather than a
ruin: nobody has been running any of it.

How this lands is not settled and can still move — it has to be earned rather
than announced, and the acts leading to it will change as it firms up.

**The fourth wall.** Once it is all worked out — the enigma solved, the
explanation given — someone stops addressing the character. A character, or the
narrator, turns and speaks to the person holding the controls: not "my lord",
but you, the one who has been here the whole time. It is the last beat, and it
only works once.

## Open

Not settled. Some of it is act 4's, some of it is a decision waiting to be made.

**How each officer is woken.** Taking the object out of a man's hand works once
and gets repetitive the third time. Candidates, all of them still "the player
handles what is theirs", each shaped by the building:

- **The Steward** — as written: take the clipboard off the tray he is holding
  out. He is offering it; the player only has to accept. Keep this one.
- **The Herald** — the tower is the clock. Rather than pulling the book from
  under his arm, the player touches the stopped thing the room is built around:
  the pendulum, the winding key, the hands on the face. The clock takes a step,
  the bell finishes its toll, and the Herald comes down the last stair as if
  nothing happened. The book is then handed over, which also fixes why he is
  carrying the player's book at all.
- **The Gatekeeper** — the gate. Something has to be let in or picked up: the
  player lifts the crate his hand is resting on, or takes the note off the top
  of the pile, or lifts the bar on the gate itself and the noise of the outside
  arrives with the man.
- Pattern, if one is wanted: **accept, wind, open**. First the player receives,
  then they set something running again, then they open a way in. Each officer
  wakes to a different verb, and the verbs are the game's own.

**Today's page.** It contradicts the freeze. If the book of hours writes on its
own while the Herald holds it, and the Herald and his tower were stopped, it
could not have written today. Two ways out:

- **The page is blank too.** The struck-through list goes, and the player's first
  four moves are written only in the chronicle, which was awake on the bedroom
  floor. Cheapest, and it costs the scene its best line.
- **The objects woke with the player, not with their officers.** The waking
  starts in the bedroom, and the books begin keeping again from that moment —
  which is exactly why today's page has today on it and nothing before it, and
  why the lines were "written in advance" only in the sense that the book writes
  a task the instant it exists. Costs nothing, explains the page, and makes the
  chronicle's first line and the book's first page the same event.

**The roll's shape.** Whether the day's roll is one flat list or is split into
morning, afternoon and night. A split gives the Steward something to hand over
three times a day; a flat list keeps the hours entirely the Herald's.

**Still unknown in the fiction.** What stopped the kingdom and how long ago;
whether anyone is awake anywhere else; what lies past the gate at all. Nobody
who has woken so far knows any of it.

**Act 4 (The Gates).** Still an outline, so nothing in it is canon yet — the
Gatekeeper's pose included. What it owes: the last note left before the
disappearance, never processed, carrying a **warning** rather than a task —
something like "i think i am losing control" — which the Gatekeeper had no shelf
for, so it stayed. And the Gate as the place where everything stops on its way
in: a note is what the player writes to themselves, a parcel is what arrives
from outside, and both wait there.
