# Act 3 — The Tower

I leave the Palace by a side door, and the air outside is the first good thing of the day. Smell of green, of wet earth.

The courtyard is open ground taken over by tall grass, knee-high, with the stones showing only where the path was most trodden. A well in the middle, the rotted rope hanging. An overturned wheelbarrow, rusted the color of dirt.

I cross slowly, parting the grass with my hands, and the tower grows in front of me. Up close it is bigger than it looked from the balcony. Ivy climbing one side, working into the cracks in the stone. The clock on its face, the hands stopped, the paint of the numbers flaking off.

The door is pushed to, not locked. I push, and it groans.

Inside it is dark and colder than outside. A stone stair spirals up, hugging the wall, no rail on the inner side. Narrow slits let in threads of light here and there.

And what bothers me before anything else is the silence. Because in a bell tower, silence is wrong.

I climb. Some fifteen steps, a full turn, my footfalls echoing. Another turn. The light comes and goes.

And then I run straight into a man.

Stopped, a hand's width from me, in the middle of a step. Coming down. One foot in the air, a thick book under his arm, the other hand out for a rail that is not on that side of the stair. Eyes open, fixed on nothing.

I jump and catch the wall so I do not fall backward.

I call out. Nothing. I wave my hand in front of his eyes. Nothing.

And it is looking over his shoulder, high above, that I see the bell. Stopped in the middle of a toll, tipped past the top of its swing. No rope pulled taut, nothing holding it. Simply stopped where nothing stops.

Then I remember the Steward. It worked once, when I took a thing out of his hand.

I get close. I hesitate, because it is a strange thing to take something out of the hand of a stopped man. And I pull the book.

And the bell finishes its toll.

Up above, a few meters off, inside the closed stone. The sound bursts out, fills the whole tower, sets the floor shaking and brings the dust down from the ceiling in a curtain. I shout and curl up. The sound does not end, it stays in there, humming in the stone.

When I open my eyes, he is awake. He staggers on the step, steadies himself on the wall, coughs on the dust. He looks up, at the bell still swinging. He looks at me.

And, in the middle of all that, he makes an automatic bow, before understanding anything at all.

Then he looks at his own empty hand. Then at the book with me. He frowns.

> — Forgive me, my lord. I do not know why I had it. It is yours.

I open it. A ribbon marks today. And today's page has four lines, all struck through.

> ~~Wake up~~
> ~~Find the chronicle~~
> ~~Say my name~~
> ~~Speak with the Steward~~

Written beforehand, in the infinitive, the way you note down what is still to be done. And struck through afterward, one by one, as I did them.

I ask who wrote that.

And he answers with the same bored matter-of-factness as the other one: nobody, my lord. The book writes. It is the book of hours, that is what it is for.

And then he shows me the difference, because to him it is obvious and to me it is not. The chronicle tells what happened. The book of hours keeps the when: what starts, when it starts, how long it lasts, when it ends.

And the bell, he says, pointing up with his chin, rings because it is written here. He is not the one who rings it. He never was.

I ask whether I can change things. He says yes, carefully: what has not come yet, my lord sets and unsets at will. What has already passed, my lord only corrects. If the book noted that you began at nine and it was in truth ten to nine, say so, and I will put it right. The book does not always follow you closely.

And, he adds, the chronicle will record that my lord corrected it. It records everything, your corrections included.

I leaf backward. Blank. Forward. Blank.

And he gets more frightened than I am. He begs my leave, takes the book back, leafs through fast, searches, turns handfuls of pages.

> — This is not your calendar, my lord. Your calendar was full. Too full, if I may say so.

And then he tells me what he does not know. He does not know what happened. He does not know how long ago. He looks through a slit, at the grass taking the courtyard below, and goes quiet.

But he knows whom to ask.

> — The Gatekeeper, my lord. He sees everything that comes in and everything that goes out. If anything passed these walls, it passed through him.

And on the clipboard in my hand, below the struck-through line, a new one appears pale, waiting for me to accept it:

> see the Gatekeeper
